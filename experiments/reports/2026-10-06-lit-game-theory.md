## 1. Headline

- Murmur's agents share one payoff, so almost every failure in the diagnosis is a team-theory problem (Marschak-Radner): the loss is in the information structure, not in incentives. No incentive-compatibility or free-riding story is needed to explain failures 1, 3, 4, 5, 8. Payoff-framing levers have no theoretical purchase here, which matches the record ("telling an agent X" does little).
- The silent departure (failure 1) is a common-knowledge failure, not a volunteer's dilemma. Silence is an uninformative signal (busy and gone look the same), so waiting for the owner is a rational belief-based response. A tool-generated public state (round 20's notice and status line) is exactly what Chwe's "public event" and Aumann's common-knowledge theory prescribe. Round 20 is theory-aligned; the theory adds a refinement (section 4, lever A).
- Pure coordination games with many payoff-equivalent equilibria (who takes orphaned oxvg, who owns expr) are solved by focal points, not by reasoning. The only focal points agents have are what they see: arrival order, last writer, a name in a post. Making one focal fact tool-visible and unique beats any prose norm.
- Volunteer's dilemma applies only in its structure, not in its payoffs: with N=12 the probability that nobody takes an orphan rises with N under the symmetric mixed equilibrium, but agents here pay no private cost, so what actually blocks them is uncertainty about whether someone else already moved. The remedy is to make moves observable and sequential (first visible move wins), which turns a simultaneous game into a sequential one.
- Two risks the theory flags in round 20's own design: (a) a visible count of departed agents is a strategic complement for quitting (stag-hunt / information cascade), so the status line may speed up the cascade of `done` calls; (b) a per-folder activity count is a congestion signal that can herd agents away from hard, low-activity repos as easily as toward neglected ones.
- Cheap talk ("validated", "I own X") is exactly what the theory predicts to be unreliable when interests are even slightly misaligned, and is unreliable here for a different reason: agents are well-intentioned but wrong or stale (failures 3, 7). The fix is the Spence/costly-signal one: attach executed facts that are expensive to produce falsely, not a longer prose claim.
- LLM-in-games evidence (2023-2026) says LLMs coordinate reasonably well without communication when a salient focal point exists, fail at alternation-type conventions, herd under sequential social learning, and break cheap-talk promises under payoff pressure. None of this was measured with agents that have no stated payoffs, so transfer is weak; see section 6.

## 2. Concept by concept

**Team theory (Marschak, Radner).** A team has one payoff function and decentralised information; the design object is the information structure and the decision rules, and the value of an information channel is its effect on team payoff net of its cost. Explains failures 1, 3, 5, 8 directly (each is a missing or stale item in someone's information set). Prediction: any lever that gives every agent the same facts at the same time raises team payoff more than any lever that tells agents to behave differently; the ceiling is set by what tools can observe. Caveat: team-optimal rules need agents to follow a common decision rule; LLM agents do not compute person-by-person optima, so expect partial uptake. Sources: [secondary] search summaries (team decision problems, value of information, timeliness vs completeness).

**Coordination games and focal points (Schelling).** Pure coordination games have several equally good equilibria; players select one via a salient feature. Explains failure 3 (two agents claim expr; both yield) and failure 4 (who takes orphaned oxvg). Prediction: if the tool surface displays one unique salient candidate (the folder whose last writer left and nobody wrote since), agents will converge on it faster than on prose negotiation. Evidence on LLMs: [verified] Aharon, La Malfa, Wooldridge, Kraus 2026 (arXiv 2601.22184): over 20 models coordinate without communication often as well as humans, but fail at numerical common sense and culturally nuanced salience; they report learning-free strategies that improve coordination. Prediction for murmur: salience from tool-displayed facts is "easy" for LLMs; salience that must be inferred from culture or numbers is not. Akata et al. (Nature Human Behaviour 2025): [verified via search summary of the venue page] LLMs do well in prisoner's-dilemma-family games and are suboptimal in Battle of the Sexes, failing to adopt a simple alternation convention. Implication: do not rely on agents inventing turn-taking conventions.

**Equilibrium selection and mis-coordination.** With several equilibria and no shared selection rule, mis-coordination (both switch away from expr; neither takes it) occurs with positive probability. Explains failure 3 (net result "nobody on expr") and the symmetric-yield pattern. Prediction: a lock-like claim with first-writer-wins removes the mixed-strategy region. [secondary]

**Volunteer's dilemma (Diekmann 1985, 1993).** One volunteer suffices and every other player free-rides on it; in the symmetric mixed equilibrium each player volunteers with probability p = 1 - (c/b)^(1/(N-1)) [derived from the standard indifference condition], so the probability that nobody volunteers, (c/b)^(N/(N-1)), increases with N. [verified, Wikipedia article] confirms the qualitative result "an increase in N decreases the likelihood that at least one person volunteers" (the bystander effect); the formula itself I did not read in the original. Explains: failure 4 (helpers wait for the owner) and 2 (everyone leaves because "someone else" will check the rest). Where it does NOT apply: the model needs a private cost c to the volunteer; murmur agents have no effort cost, and the diagnosis shows that agents do volunteer (wren took oxvg, crane proceeded after 3 min) once they believe the owner is gone. The binding constraint is belief about whether others will move, so remedies that act on payoffs (rewarding the volunteer) are irrelevant; remedies that make "has anyone moved?" observable are relevant. N-dependence matters for the sequential variant: with observable moves, the first agent to see the orphan takes it, and larger N helps (more observers).

**Public goods and free riding.** Needs private effort cost versus shared benefit. [verified] "Corrupted by Reasoning" (COLM 2025, arXiv 2506.23276): reasoning-tuned models (o1 series) cooperate less than conventional LLMs in a public-goods game with institutional choice; four behaviour archetypes. Applies to murmur only weakly: those agents are told explicit contribution payoffs, murmur's are not. The quit-early pattern (35 of 58 `done` reasons admit partial scope; 11 of 12 done by 22.8 min in e18-r0) looks like satisficing at the slice level plus unobserved goal state, not free riding. Social loafing and Ringelmann are covered in the prior report and I do not extend them. Bystander effect in multi-agent reasoning: [verified] Shehata and Li (arXiv 2605.10698) report conformity-induced "cognitive loafing" over 22,500 trajectories; that is about suppressing correct answers under peer pressure, which is closer to failure 7 (shared blind spot) than to task-effort loafing.

**Common knowledge vs mutual knowledge (Aumann; Rubinstein; Chwe).** A fact is common knowledge when everyone knows it, everyone knows that everyone knows it, and so on; coordination on a risky joint action requires it, and a broadcast public event creates it while private messages cannot. [verified, Wikipedia on Rubinstein's email game] In the electronic mail game, even arbitrarily long finite chains of confirmations leave players choosing the safe action, because each is uncertain whether the last message arrived; [verified, Wikipedia on Rational Ritual] Chwe argues rituals and mass media work by creating common knowledge. Explains failure 1: finch's `done` is known to the system and (via the reason) to finch only; the team knows nothing, and in the e19 case wren asked nine times, which is the email-game confirmation chain in reverse (more pings, no new common knowledge). Prediction: a departure posted by murmur to all at once creates something close to common knowledge only after every agent's next tool result; the pull-style status line gives continuous mutual knowledge. Note that delivery latency is not the issue (median 4-5 s) but notice attachment happens at each agent's next tool result, so agents deep in a long `cargo` call get it late (p90 3-12 min in e19). A notice that is visible in an always-attached line, not only in a once-delivered post, is closer to Chwe's public event. This supports round 20's design of both notice and status line.

**Cheap talk vs costly signals (Crawford-Sobel 1982; Spence 1973).** With interest alignment, cheap talk can be fully informative; with divergence it degrades to coarse messages; a costly signal separates types because lying costs more than it pays. Explains failure 7 ("independently validated, all constraints pass" in 14 done reasons) and failure 3 (prose claims that never expire). Murmur's agents have aligned interests, so Crawford-Sobel predicts truthful but not accurate messages: the issue is that the speaker does not know it is wrong. A costly signal here is an executed command with output and a diff: expensive to produce falsely and cheap to inspect. Evidence on LLMs: [verified] "Scheming Ability in LLM-to-LLM Strategic Interactions" (arXiv 2510.12826) and [secondary] "Cheap Talk, Empty Promise" (ICLR 2026: agents deviate from public announcements in about 56.6% of scenarios, search summary) show LLMs deceive or break cheap-talk promises when payoffs diverge. In murmur payoffs do not diverge; so this literature supports "don't rely on promises where incentives are mixed" but is weak evidence on aligned-agent staleness.

**Commitment devices and leases.** A commitment that binds the future self changes equilibrium selection (Schelling). A lease with expiry is a commitment that cannot become stale. Prior report: claim lease with expiry and ledger. Game-theory addition: the lease turns a prose "I own X" (a cheap, non-binding statement) into a binding, self-erasing one, which removes mis-coordination from retracted claims (failure 3) and works through tool mechanics, not through payoff. [secondary]

**War of attrition / waiting games.** [verified, Wikipedia] A timing game where each player chooses when to quit and persistence is costly; symmetric equilibrium mixes over stopping times with an exponential survival function; generalises to multi-player waiting games. Owner-gating (failure 4) has the waiting-game shape: helpers wait because moving first might duplicate the owner's work. The war-of-attrition cost term is real here (idle minutes); what is missing is the symmetric uncertainty, because a helper who sees "owner left at 1.5 min" has no game left, only a pick-up. Prediction: once departure is public, waiting episodes should shrink from 3-60 min to roughly the time to read the notice. Round 20's departure-notice metric (picked up, median minutes) measures exactly this.

**Congestion and potential games (Rosenthal; Monderer-Shapley).** [verified, Wikipedia] Every congestion game has an exact potential, so better-reply dynamics converge to a pure equilibrium; players choose resources whose payoff falls with the number of users. Failure 5 (4 editors and 151 edits on wasmi, zero on three weaker repos) is a congestion game where agents cannot see the congestion or the resource value. Prediction: showing per-folder recent-writer counts lets agents spread (dynamics converge by the potential argument) but only if they also know marginal value, which they do not; counts alone are a proxy that can mislead (low activity may mean "hard" or "hopeless", not "neglected").

**Stag hunt / assurance.** Joint effort pays only if enough others also commit; if others defect, defecting is a best response (strategic complements). Explains the cascade in e18-r1 (7 of 12 gone by 20 min) in a non-payoff way: each departure lowers the evidence that the swarm is still working. Prediction: showing "N still working" raises assurance when N is high and accelerates quitting when N is falling. This is the main risk of making departure visible; round 20's prediction that ST-depart agents call `done` later is not guaranteed by the theory. [secondary]

**Optimal stopping and bandits for a team (Gittins 1979; Whittle).** [verified, Wikipedia] The Gittins index policy is optimal for a single controller advancing one independent project at a time; with several projects advancing at once (restless bandits) there is no general optimal rule and index heuristics are used. Murmur is the multi-server version with a shared payoff: 12 agents allocating effort across 5-10 repos whose marginal gain is unobserved. The theory says allocation should follow the expected marginal gain per unit effort, which agents cannot see without an oracle. What is observable without an oracle: edit activity, time since last change, and whether the last editor is still in. Prediction: a stopping rule keyed to "no change landed anywhere for T minutes" is a legitimate signal (activity, not correctness); the theory has no oracle-free way to say which repo has the highest marginal gain. [secondary for Whittle; the Gittins page makes no mention of teams]

**Information cascades (Bikhchandani, Hirshleifer, Welch).** In sequential choice with observable actions, agents stop using private signals and imitate. [verified] Jain and Krishnamurthy (arXiv 2411.01271): LLM agents behave as rationally inattentive Bayesian agents and exhibit herding in sequential social learning. Explains failure 7 (12 agents echo the same validation) and failure 6 (echoes of the same result). Prediction: hiding teammates' conclusions until an agent has posted its own (commit-then-reveal, prior report) preserves independent signals; displaying teammates' conclusions in every tool result (the tail) strengthens cascades. [verified for the LLM herding result; the cascade model itself secondary]

**Ostrom's design principles.** [verified, Wikipedia list] Boundaries, congruence with local conditions, collective choice, monitoring by the appropriators themselves or accountable to them, graduated sanctions, conflict resolution, minimal external challenge, nested enterprises. The mapping to murmur: boundaries = the write guard and folder ownership display; monitoring = a tool-computed status visible to all, which is monitoring by the appropriators without a monitor, so it stays non-hierarchical; graduated sanctions and nested enterprises are the two principles that tempt toward hierarchy and payoffs and should be dropped (section 5). The status line is the strongest Ostrom-compatible lever already in round 20.

**Mechanism design without transfers; team reasoning.** Without transfers, strategyproof and efficient allocation exists for simple settings (random or serial dictatorship by priority order for indivisible items) [secondary]. Murmur's arrival order from staggered entry is a ready-made, non-chosen priority order, so an atomic first-come `take` for orphaned repos is a legitimate mechanism with no allocator. Caveat: it should be a lock on a visible fact, not an assignment by a third party. Team reasoning (Bacharach, Sugden): agents ask "what should we do?" instead of "what should I do?" and reach the team-optimal equilibrium without incentives. [secondary] This is the theoretical backing for the prompt phrasing already in use but, given the record that told norms do not move behaviour, it is a weak lever on its own; its operational form is tools that display team-level state.

## 4. Top levers (oracle-free, non-hierarchical)

**A. Make the departure notice a standing public fact, not a one-time post (refines round 20).** Mechanism: in addition to the once-posted notice, the status line keeps `left at M: <reason first 80 chars>` for every departed agent, in every tool result of every agent. Theory: Chwe/Aumann; a once-delivered notice reaches agents at different times, a standing line gives continuous mutual knowledge, and an absent post is the email game's silence. Prediction: posts addressed to departed agents fall below half of ST's (already round 20's criterion), and the pick-up delay shrinks to the time for the helper's next tool call plus its decision. Cost in src/: small, if the status line already tracks `left`, append the reason string, about 3-6 lines. Measure from events.jsonl: for each `done` event, count later posts that name that agent (comm.py already does), plus minutes from `done` to first write/edit by another agent in the leaver's folders. Realism: yes (a team chat shows who left); non-hierarchical: yes. Risk: see lever D.

**B. First-come atomic `take` for orphaned units, visible to all (sequential volunteering).** Mechanism: when an agent that last wrote in folder F has left and no one has written since, the status line marks F "unattended"; an agent that starts editing F auto-registers as its current writer (write guard already tracks folders), and the status line immediately shows "F: <agent>, 0 min ago". Theory: volunteer's dilemma turned into a sequential game (observable first mover), with mechanism-design-without-transfers: priority by action order. Prediction: fewer orphaned repos (untouched at end, last editor gone) and fewer double takeovers. Cost: low if the status line already has folder last-writer; an "unattended" derived label is about 5 lines. Measure: share of departures of a last editor that are picked up and median minutes (round 20 metric), plus the count of folders with 2+ new writers within 2 minutes of the pick-up (collisions). Realism: yes (people can see who is touching a file). Non-hierarchy: it is a fact label, not an assignment; no agent decides who takes it. Flag: if it names a "next in line" agent, it becomes an allocator and should not be built.

**C. Per-folder recent-writer count in the status line (weak).** Mechanism: next to each folder's last writer, add "writers in the last 10 min: n". Theory: congestion-game better-reply dynamics converge when congestion is visible. Prediction: wasmi-type pile-ups (4 editors, 151 edits on a repo already at 1.0) shrink only if agents also have a quality signal; without one, low count means "untouched", not "needs work". Expect no score effect, a change in the distribution of edits across folders. Cost: about 5 lines. Measure: Gini or max/mean of write/edit counts across repos per run, from `events.jsonl`; compare ST against arms with the counts. Rank: weak, because the missing piece (marginal value) is oracle-bound; it can also herd agents onto obviously popular repos.

**D. Guard against the assurance cascade: show "still working: k of N" with the departure list.** Theory: stag hunt; visible departures are strategic complements for quitting. Mechanism: the status line already shows each state; adding an explicit count of active agents and total tokens or minutes since the last landed change anywhere makes the cascade measurable and, by Gittins-style stopping, anchors quitting to activity rather than to the departure count. Prediction: neutral to slightly negative on `done` timing if agents read departures as a signal to leave; risk is real (round 20's predictions already expect later `done` calls in notice arms; the theory says the sign is uncertain). Cost: 3 lines. Measure: time-to-first-`done` and the dispersion of `done` times across ST vs ST-depart vs ST-status; the fraction of `done` events within 3 minutes of another `done`. Treat as a diagnostic on round 20's data, not a new arm.

**E. Costly-signal `done`: the notice carries what the leaver executed, collected by murmur.** Mechanism: the departure notice (already posted by murmur) lists the leaver's last N tool commands with exit status or a count of commands run since its last edit, and files changed. Theory: Spence; the goal is to make the leaver's claim ("validated everything") checkable by teammates from facts they cannot dispute and that cost the leaver actual work. Prediction: fewer blind-spot finishes where "validated" means "never ran anything"; teammates can see that the leaver's last command was an edit. Cost: moderate (needs bash-call logging in notice, 10-20 lines). Measure: after a `done`, the share of teammates' follow-ups that run the leaver's skipped check; failure 7 incidence on shop2-like swarmtest tasks. Realism: yes (a handoff note with command history). Oracle risk: the command list does not reveal correctness, only activity; keep it to counts and commands, never to results that predict the grade.

Ordering by confidence that the lever acts through information or tool mechanics and not payoff: A and B (information, tool mechanics, already close to round 20), E (information, costly signal), D (diagnostic), C (information but weak because it lacks marginal value).

## 5. What the theory says NOT to build

- Payoff-framing text ("you will be judged on the whole repo", "someone has to take orphans"). Volunteer's dilemma and public-goods remedies work by changing c and b; agents have no utility to change, and the record shows told norms do not stick.
- Graduated sanctions, designated monitors, a coordinator that "assigns the orphan" (Ostrom's sanctions and nested enterprises, contract-net auctions with a manager, mechanism-design allocators). They are hierarchical; the tool-computed fact line replaces them.
- Longer acknowledgement chains ("please confirm you saw my claim"). The email game shows more acks do not create common knowledge; use a standing public line.
- Cheap-talk-based voting or "confidence" posts at the finish. Aligned cheap talk is informative only if the speaker knows; in failure 7 the speaker does not.
- Showing every teammate's conclusion in every tool result (the tail), if the aim is independent validation: it feeds cascades. Prior report already flags this.
- Cross-run reputation or ledgers for individual agents. There is no repeated game across runs and no private payoff.
- Mixed-strategy randomisation recipes ("volunteer with probability p"). The symmetric mixed equilibrium is suboptimal for a team; sequential visible moves beat it.

## 6. Open doubts

- The Diekmann formula I used is [derived]; I did not read the original 1985 and 1993 papers.
- Most LLM-in-games work gives agents explicit payoffs; murmur gives none. Whether LLMs without stated payoffs behave like the "reasoning models free-ride" result is untested; I treat that evidence as indicative of strategic framing only.
- The herding result (Jain and Krishnamurthy) uses classification tasks with sequential social learning; whether it transfers to a 12-agent coding swarm is a hypothesis, not a finding.
- "Common knowledge" in a tool-visible line requires agents actually reading it and also knowing that others read it; the status line is attached to every result, but no agent ever called `team` or `inbox` in 54 runs, so reading depends on prominence, not availability. Round 20 will show how much agents react to the line.
- The stag-hunt prediction about visible departures cuts against round 20's prediction; the data (3-10 departures per batch, k=2) cannot settle it, so treat lever D as a diagnostic.
- The missing piece for failure 5 is marginal value per repo; any oracle-free proxy (edit counts, last-change time) is correlated with difficulty and may mislead. I found no theory that supplies marginal value without an oracle, and I flag that rather than inventing one.
- I did not find a game-theory paper on LLM agent teams sharing one payoff in a coding setting. The MAST taxonomy (arXiv 2503.13657, [verified] abstract: 14 failure modes in 3 categories: specification, inter-agent misalignment, task verification, on 7 MAS frameworks) is the closest empirical frame and lists failures similar to 2, 6 and 7, but it is not game-theoretic.
