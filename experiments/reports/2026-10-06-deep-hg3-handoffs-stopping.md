## 1 Headline

1. **No study isolates which handoff component carries the effect.** I-PASS was tested as a bundle (mnemonic, training, faculty observation, campaign) in a pre-post design with no control arm [abstract]. The only decompositions are indirect: which content elements rose after the bundle (illness severity 0% to 19%, contingency planning 24% to 41%) and a meta-analysis in which protocols with 12 or more items pass more information but can lengthen handovers and raise omission errors [abstract]. Any claim that "the action list" or "read-back" is the active ingredient is inference.
2. **The strongest hard-evidence results are about the receiver's state, not the leaver's message.** In air traffic control, 15-18% of all operational errors fell into each of the first three 10-minute intervals after a position change, and decades of checklist improvement did not reverse it [full text, FAA AM-08/16 p. 1, quoting Lowry et al. 2005]. In a 50-participant lab experiment, written notes alone left 6.2 of 8 bolts wrong versus 3.5 with face-to-face and about 3.6-4.0 with audio or video; the "no handover at all" control had 4.8, no better than paper alone [full text, Parke et al., Results]. A written handover can be worth little; the state left in the workspace carried information.
3. **The visible clock is not what the pacing literature links to early exit.** Gersick's eight teams paused and changed approach at the calendar midpoint, triggered by awareness of time rather than work done, then accelerated to a final burst [full text, pp. 27-28, 34]. The only team that stopped early (Group G) did so because its leader declared the task complete [full text, p. 27]. Early, near-synchronous exit in murmur looks more like need for closure plus a homogeneous group than like pacing.
4. **Handoff hazards are mostly after the handoff, and human HROs do not use read-back.** In four high-consequence settings (NASA mission control, nuclear, rail, ambulance dispatch) every one of 21 handoff strategies was seen somewhere except read-back and fixed ordering [full text, Patterson p. 127]. Unambiguous transfer of responsibility was used everywhere, and at-a-glance displays meant less needed saying [full text, pp. 129-130]. That is the argument for derived state over structured free text.
5. **Pre-announcing a departure can hurt.** In 32 triads, an anticipated member change reduced specialisation and, through it, performance; an unanticipated change did not [full text, Blanchet & Michinov pp. 5-7]. Do not build a "plan to leave" signal.
6. **Closure under time pressure is real but mixed.** Need for closure induced by time pressure raised conformity pressure and less egalitarian participation in 4-person role-play groups [abstract, De Grada 1999]; time pressure helped or hurt hidden-profile solving depending on the strength of initial preferences [abstract, Kelly & Karau 1999]. Independent pre-discussion dissent raised hidden-profile solution rates even when every individual preference was wrong (135 triads) [abstract, Schulz-Hardt 2006]. This favours commit-then-reveal and showing divergence, not roles.

## 2 Findings by topic

### 2.1 I-PASS and handoff bundles (FM2, FM1)

- **Starmer et al. NEJM 2014.** Prospective intervention study (pre-post, not randomised), 9 paediatric residency programmes, 10,740 admissions. Medical errors 24.5 to 18.8 per 100 admissions (-23%, P<0.001). Preventable adverse events 4.7 to 3.3 (-30%, P<0.001). Non-preventable adverse events 3.0 to 2.8 (P=0.79, no change). Significant error reduction at 6 of 9 sites. All 14 prespecified key elements (9 written, 5 oral) increased (P<0.001). Oral handoff duration 2.4 to 2.5 minutes per patient (P=0.55) [abstract, Europe PMC record; I could not open the paper]. Two secondary sources misreport the headline: PSNet says "23% reduction in preventable adverse events"; the I-PASS Institute flyer says "30 percent reduction in medical errors that harm patients" [full text, flyer p. 1]; the abstract says 23% errors and 30% preventable adverse events. Treat vendor wording as marketing.
- **What the mnemonic contains.** Illness severity; Patient summary; Action list; Situation awareness and contingency plan; Synthesis by receiver [fetch summary of Starmer 2012, PMC9923540]. The 2012 article says the three elements most often missing from resident handoffs were illness severity, contingency planning and read-back; it contains no pilot error numbers [fetch summary].
- **Pilot (Boston, one hospital, 2 units, 1,255 admissions).** Errors 33.8 to 18.3 per 100, preventable adverse events 3.3 to 1.5 [secondary: JAMA 2013 via PSNet and trade press]. The 2016 follow-up on verbal handoff quality (prospective pre-post, same two units) found a 63-point rise on a 0-500 scale (95% CI 18-108); illness severity assessment 0% to 19%; complete patient summary 34% to 47% (P=0.07); contingency 24% to 41% (P=0.009). The unit with a free-text document gained far more (151 to 247, P<0.0001) than the unit with a computerised tool (129 to 151, P=0.17) [fetch summary of PMC5504880; authors list no blinding and a short post period]. This is the nearest thing to a component contrast: structure in the *spoken* content mattered, and the tool did not add on its own.
- **32-hospital implementation study** (2,735 residents; hybrid effectiveness-implementation): major adverse events 1.7 to 0.9 per person-year, minor 17.5 to 9.3, all five verbal elements 20% to 66.3%, all five written 10.4% to 73.5%; authors report adherence to all elements together, not component effects[fetch summary, PMC10964397; unverified].
- **Meta-analysis (Keebler et al. 2016).** 36 pre-post articles from live settings; Hedges' g: handoff information 0.71 (CI .63-.79), patient outcomes 0.53 (.41-.65), provider outcomes 0.51 (.41-.60), organisational outcomes 0.29 (.23-.35); protocols with 12 or more items passed more information than those with 11 or fewer; "protocols can increase the time for handover and the rate of errors of omission"; substantial publication bias and heterogeneity [abstract].
- **Depends on what LLM agents lack:** residents' fatigue, rotation schedules, training hours, faculty observation. **What agents have:** every call logged, so content completeness of a leave note can be computed, not trained.

### 2.2 High-consequence handoffs (Patterson et al. 2004) (FM1, FM2)

- Design: secondary analysis of ethnographic data from four settings, 21 handoff strategies scored as "used routinely / as needed / not observed". Observation hours: shuttle mission control 67 (16 shift changes), nuclear 177 (7 handoffs), rail 60, ambulance dispatch 118 [full text, Table 1, p. 126]. Observers analysed their own data; the authors flag validity risks [full text, p. 126].
- Not observed anywhere: read-back (strategy 7) and a fixed order for updates (strategy 10). Everything else was seen in at least one setting [full text, p. 127]. All observed updates were interactive, face-to-face, and the leaver was inaccessible afterwards, so transfer was unambiguous [full text, p. 127].
- Strategies that need no receiver and no manager: outgoing writes a short summary (shuttle control only); incoming assesses status from at-a-glance displays; incoming scans recent log entries and automatically captured data changes; incoming gets primary access to tools and annotated forms; physical or tool-based marker of who is responsible; everyone can see who owns what at a glance; overhear others' updates [full text, pp. 128-129].
- Strategies that involve a human relationship or a schedule: face-to-face questioning, topics initiated by the incoming person, outgoing oversees the incoming person (shuttle control: a one-hour overlap), delay transfer during a critical activity (observed up to several hours once) [full text, pp. 128-130].
- Of 75 questions asked in 16 NASA handoffs, 8 were checks for error ("Do you know that for sure?") [full text, p. 125]. Fresh eyes on arrival catch fixation errors, which is a benefit of handoff, not only a cost.
- Authors' own statement: at-a-glance status displays mean the outgoing person need not convey information the display shows (ambulance positions from GPS) [full text, p. 130].

### 2.3 Shift handover experiments and operational data (FM1)

- **Parke, Hobbs & Kanki (NASA Ames).** 50 paid participants in 10 trials of 5, repeated-measures; each "shift" 10 minutes, then 5 minutes to brief the next person; five conditions (paper, paper+audio, paper+video, paper+face-to-face, cues alone). Errors (of 8 bolts): paper 6.2, face-to-face 3.5, video 3.6, audio 4.0, cues alone 4.8; paper had significantly more errors than all except cues alone (F(4,36)=3.67, p=.013). Paper-only writers produced the longest notes (390 acts versus 243-265) and still did worse. Givers asked 1.0 questions per handover; receivers 3.7 [full text, Results]. The authors conclude that the benefit was non-verbal cue transfer rather than interactive questioning, since audio and video (no questions) nearly matched face-to-face. It is a lab task with paid students, no real stakes.
- **FAA AM-08/16.** Retrospective analysis of en route operational errors, 2001-2004. Prior work: about 15-18% of operational errors in each of the first three 10-minute intervals after a position change, in en route, TRACON and tower alike. Position transfers differ by type and time pressure; the checklist is "well grounded" but "insufficient for assessing the various states of mind a controller is operating under" [full text, abstract and p. 1; I read only the front matter and introduction].
- **Software on-call and security incident response.** Six interviews (Kent et al. 2026): themes of signposting (point to the ticket rather than narrate), varied and evolving procedures, individual style; tickets and run-books did the carrying [full text, pp. 3-5; N=6, exploratory]. I found no quantitative incident-handoff study.
- Premise gap: these studies assume the leaver is forced out and replaced; murmur agents leave by choice and are not replaced.

### 2.4 Turnover and member change (FM1, FM3)

- **Blanchet & Michinov 2017.** 91 students in 32 groups (13 anticipated change, 12 unanticipated, 7 none), Meccano assembly. Anticipated membership change reduced transactive memory (b=-1.15, p=.01), mediated the effect on errors (indirect effect 0.46, 95% CI [0.12, 1.01]); no direct effect on performance (b=.17, ns). Only the specialisation component mediated (b=-1.16, p=.01). Unanticipated change had no significant effect (indirect CI [0.00, 0.64]). Authors: members who know someone will leave do not specialise, and instead learn everything [full text, pp. 5-7]. Small, lab, mostly women, six dyads in place of triads.
- **Summers, Humphrey & Ferris 2012.** Team member change caused high flux in coordination when the person moved to a strategically core role or when information transfer was low; a high-ability newcomer in a core role made flux worse [abstract].
- **Lewis, Belliveau, Herndon & Keller 2007** (not obtained): trained-together groups kept using the old transactive memory structure after partial reconstitution, and did worse than groups whose structure fit [secondary: described in Blanchet & Michinov p. 2; [memory] for effect sizes].
- **Foucault et al. 2015** (five OSS projects, 5-80 kLoC, manual bug-fix labelling): at any release at least 80% of contributors were newcomers or leavers; only 8-19% of newcomers became stayers; external newcomers' activity correlated with bug-fix density (95% bootstrap CI for the correlation spanned about 0.13-0.81), external leavers and internal turnover showed no significant relation [full text of the authors' slides, pp. 14-18; not the paper]. In plain words: arrivals taking over unfamiliar code were associated with defects; departures alone were not.
- **Depends on what LLM agents lack:** personal expertise built over time. Agents are interchangeable and identical in prompt, so what a leaver "knows" is only what it wrote or changed; the loss is local state and intent, which logs can partly reconstruct.

### 2.5 Coordination without fixed command (FM1, FM5)

- **Weick 1993 (Mann Gulch).** One case reanalysed from Maclean's book: 13 died. Conditions: unexpected fire behaviour against the crew's "10:00 fire" expectation, role system unclear ("people are often unclear who is in charge"), a foreman's escape fire not understood [full text, pp. 628-635]. Survivors: two who stayed together and one who improvised; "the rest ... took less notice of one another" and the group "disintegrated" [full text, p. 638]. Four proposed resilience sources: improvisation, virtual role systems (each person able to mentally run the other roles), wisdom (doubt your own knowledge), respectful interaction (trust, honesty, self-respect); "a partner strengthens independent judgment in the face of a majority" [full text, pp. 638-643]. This is a single reconstructed case, not an effect size, and the leader-follower structure is explicit.
- **Incident command system.** Bigley & Roberts (2001): a bureaucratic, role-based system that nevertheless produced flexible organisations, from an inductive study of one fire department [abstract]. Moynihan (2009): ICS is "a temporary hierarchical structure", the dominant US crisis mechanism; authority is shared and contested in practice [abstract]. **Hierarchy flag: ICS assigns roles through an incident commander. Not transferable as a mechanism.**
- **Fast-response teams.** Faraj & Xiao (2006), one trauma centre: expertise coordination (protocols, communities of practice, plug-and-play teaming, knowledge sharing) plus dialogic coordination (epistemic contestation, joint sensemaking, cross-boundary intervention, protocol breaking); dialogic practices are "highly contested because of epistemic differences, reputation stakes, and possible blame" [abstract]. **Flag: a trauma team has attendings and a status order.**
- **Time to notice a missing member:** I found no study that measures it. The closest data: disintegration within minutes (Weick), error clusters in the first 30 minutes after a position change (FAA), and weeks for Debian's MIA process (prior report).

### 2.6 Time, deadlines and pacing (FM7)

- **Gersick 1988.** Eight real project teams, 7 days to 6 months, observed in full with transcripts; grounded theory; four studied first, four added and results "highly consistent". Pattern: phase 1 inertia set by the first meeting; a transition at the midpoint of the calendar between first meeting and deadline "despite wide variation in the amounts of time the eight teams were allotted"; phase 2 inertia; a final burst. At the transition teams dropped old approaches, voiced urgency "at this time and no other", and made contact with outside stakeholders [full text, pp. 17, 27-28]. Mechanism proposed: problemistic search plus pacing; "the midpoint appears to work like an alarm clock... heightening members' awareness that their time is limited"; the transition is "an opportunity for, not a guarantee of, progress"; the author says the midpoint timing itself should not carry the whole weight of the findings [full text, p. 34]. Group G dissolved at its midpoint without warning, its leader announcing the task complete; members said "there was a need to go up. But instead of going up, we stopped" [full text, p. 27]. Limits: N=8, no comparison group, known deadlines, external supervisors, post hoc grounded coding.
- **Waller, Zellmer-Bruhn & Giambatista 2002.** 38 groups under stable or changed deadlines: attention to time rose steadily as the deadline neared rather than jumping at the midpoint, yet task transitions still happened at or near the midpoint [abstract].
- **Kelly & Karau 1999.** Triads on a hidden-profile drug decision; initial preferences "major determinants of group decisions"; time pressure improved or worsened quality depending on the strength of initial preferences and on the interaction content [abstract].
- **De Grada et al. 1999.** Two studies, four-person role-play committees; need for closure (dispositional, and induced by time pressure in Study 1) went with more task-oriented and fewer positive social acts; high-need groups showed greater conformity pressure and less egalitarian participation [abstract].
- **Kruglanski et al. 2006.** High need for closure, dispositional or situational, gives "group-centrism": uniformity pressure, autocratic leadership, in-group favouritism, rejecting deviates, resistance to change [abstract].
- **Depends on what LLM agents lack:** anxiety, social comparison, deadlines imposed by outsiders. **What they have:** exact elapsed and remaining time as a number, always in view.

### 2.7 Premature consensus, groupthink and escalation (FM6, FM7)

- **Groupthink evidence.** Aldag & Fuller (1993) review: laboratory studies mostly fail to support cohesiveness as a driver; directive leaders produced fewer alternatives and discussion (Leana 1985); decision procedures changed decision time but not quality (Callaway et al. 1985); measurement was weak [full text, pp. 535-538]. Turner & Pratkanis 1998: poor decisions predicted by Janis "rarely documented", cohesiveness and directive leadership had few effects [secondary]. Esser 1998 ("alive and well") finds insulation, impartial leadership and unanimity rules linked to worse process [secondary]. Murmur has no cohesion, no leader and no stress; the live variable is identical priors in every member.
- **Dissent.** Schulz-Hardt et al. 2006: 135 triads, personnel selection with four alternatives, hidden profile. Groups with homogeneous suboptimal preferences "hardly ever" solved it; groups with prediscussion dissent did so significantly more often "even if none of these individual prediscussion preferences were correct"; magnitude of dissent did not matter; effect mediated by discussion intensity [abstract]. Mojzisch & Schulz-Hardt (2010) found that knowing others' preferences beforehand degraded decisions [memory].
- **Escalation of commitment** (the opposite error). Sleesman et al. 2012 meta-analysis: sunk costs ρ=.243 (k=34), proximity to project completion ρ=.393, ego threat ρ=.473 (k=8), shared decision authority raised the responsibility effect (.490, k=4, versus .257, k=45); "escalation in group contexts has received little attention" [full text, approx. pp. 545-554; page numbers read off running heads]. Murmur's agents stop early, so this is a warning against over-correcting rather than a model of the problem. Note that nearness to completion pushes toward persistence, which sits oddly with early exit and suggests agents believe they are "complete".

## 3 Answers to the four key questions

**(a) What makes a handoff reliable; which components carry the effect; what can a tool provide?** No study isolates a component. Convergent weak evidence: a fixed content structure including priority and contingencies (Starmer pilot content rises; Keebler g=.71 for information, but 12+ items risk omissions and time); an interactive or rich channel (Parke, but mostly via cue transfer); unambiguous ownership transfer and at-a-glance state (Patterson, all settings); incoming review of recorded changes (Patterson strategies 11-12). Read-back, the presumed active ingredient of I-PASS, was not seen in any of the four HRO settings [full text, Patterson p. 127]. A tool can supply without a manager: derived state, automatic change logs shown to the arriver, a clear and visible owner marker, required fields. It cannot supply a live receiver's confirmation unless a teammate answers.

**(b) How do groups notice a missing member and redistribute, and how long?** No time-to-notice measurement exists in what I read. Weick shows disintegration when members stop noticing each other, within about 16 minutes at Mann Gulch [full text, p. 629 timeline, p. 638]. The FAA shows risk concentrated in the first 30 minutes after a transfer. Blanchet & Michinov show that groups hit by an unannounced change simply carried on and performed like unchanged groups in the lab task. Human organisations rely on a person whose job is relief (controller, supervisor); murmur has none.

**(c) Does a clock cause synchronised stopping or a midpoint transition?** The evidence supports a midpoint reorientation (Gersick; Waller qualifies the mechanism to steady attention with transition near the midpoint), then a closing burst. It does not support clock-driven synchronised exit; the one early stopper in Gersick had a leader calling it. Need-for-closure studies (De Grada, Kruglanski) add that real time pressure raises conformity pressure, and that is the closer fit for 12 agents ending on the same weak criterion. Time pressure is not uniformly harmful (Kelly & Karau). All of this is for humans with fixed external deadlines; murmur's budget is a run budget, not a deliverable date.

**(d) Structures that counter premature consensus without a leader or an assigned devil's advocate?** The best-supported are structural: independent preferences formed and recorded before they are visible, so that dissent exists at the start (Schulz-Hardt, 135 triads); keep who-thinks-what visible as divergence, not as agreement counts; and no directive voice (Leana: directive leaders propose fewer alternatives). Assigned devil's advocacy is an assigned role and is excluded; Nemeth's finding that authentic dissent beats assigned dissent is [memory].

## 4 What transfers and what does not

| Finding | Needs from humans that agents lack | Agents have that humans lack | Transfers as |
|---|---|---|---|
| Structured handoff content (I-PASS, SBAR) | training, faculty observation, a culture campaign | every action is logged; completeness is computable | required fields filled from the log, not from the leaver's memory |
| Read-back / synthesis by receiver | a live, attentive receiver | none | weak; HROs did not use it |
| Face-to-face and cues (Parke) | physical presence | the workspace itself is the cue; all files are readable | the shared folder already carries "cues"; make cues more legible |
| Position-relief hazard window (FAA) | fatigue, shift pressure | exact time since last departure | information flag during the window |
| At-a-glance status (Patterson) | display design | state can be computed exactly | team status line (round 20) and its extension |
| Anticipated departures hurt (Blanchet) | relationships, reliance on a known leaver | agents hold no relationships | avoid pre-announcement of exit |
| Midpoint transition (Gersick) | an external deadline, supervisors | exact clock | a neutral elapsed/remaining fact; possibly a midpoint marker |
| Need for closure (De Grada, Kruglanski) | anxiety, social rejection of deviates | a uniform prior (same model and prompt) | the mechanism is probably prior homogeneity, not emotion |
| Prediscussion dissent (Schulz-Hardt) | different individuals with different views | cheap independent attempts; the same model with sampling variance | commit-then-reveal, divergence display |

## 5 Where the field disagrees or results are fragile

- **I-PASS has no component test.** Pre-post, no concurrent control at the 9 sites; "medical errors" came from active surveillance by staff who knew about the programme [abstract]; two secondary sources misstate the headline numbers. The multicentre follow-up numbers I have are from a summariser and are unchecked.
- **Longer protocols are better and worse.** Keebler: 12 or more items pass more information, yet protocols lengthened handovers and raised omissions [abstract]. Whether murmur's `done` should get more fields or fewer is open.
- **Read-back.** I-PASS builds in synthesis by receiver; Patterson found read-back in no HRO setting. Observational data, but a direct tension.
- **Time and midpoints.** Gersick's exact midpoint (N=8) versus Waller's steady attention (38 groups, abstract only); the author herself says timing should not carry the full weight [full text, p. 34]. I did not read Okhuysen & Waller 2002 on boundary conditions.
- **Time pressure helps or harms** by initial preference strength (Kelly & Karau) and by group (De Grada); my abstract-level reading cannot say which applies to agents.
- **Turnover.** Departures versus quality: Foucault finds no significant leaver effect but a newcomer effect; Summers, Lewis and Blanchet find costs of change that depend on role, information transfer and anticipation. Directions differ across levels (code, lab, firm).
- **Groupthink.** Janis's model has weak laboratory support (Aldag & Fuller; Turner & Pratkanis), while Esser's title claims it is alive; I could not read Esser. The relevant lessons (dissent, directive leaders) survive the critique; the cohesiveness claim does not.

## 6 Levers (default off, facts only, no assignment, no test signal)

Relation to the earlier lever list (L1 bounce on a post to a departed agent, L2 leave-time orphan view, L3 structured `done`, L4 take-over on lapse): none of these repeats them; M1 and M3 extend L2 and the status line.

**M1. Relief-window flag (information).** For the first W minutes (say 15) after any agent's `done`, the team status view marks that agent's folders and claims: "changed hands X minutes ago; last write Y; N writes by others since; unanswered messages to X: K". Basis: errors cluster after handovers, not at departure [FAA p. 1] and the arriver, not the leaver, is the weak point. Prediction: more reads of the leaver's files and exit note in the window; fewer overwrites of the leaver's last edits. Measure from `events.jsonl`: for each `done`, the count of reads/writes by others to that agent's folders in minutes 0-15 versus 15-30, and re-edits of the leaver's last-edited lines. Cost: small, reuses the status data. Not oracle-based.

**M2. Arrival brief on first write into a departed agent's area (tool).** The first edit or `take` by another agent returns the leaver's exit note and its last 5 writes (diff summaries). Basis: Patterson strategies 11-12 (incoming reviews logged changes before acting) and strategy 15 (primary access to tools and annotations). It is a tool output, not prompt text. Prediction: fewer clobbering edits; faster productive first edit. Measure: time from first read of the brief to the next edit; reverts of the leaver's edits within 10 minutes. Cost: 20-30 lines in the write guard; overlaps L4 if both are built, so build one.

**M3. Divergence view at finish (information).** When an agent is about to call `done`, its tool result shows, for each other agent's stated finish reason, only the distinct criteria named (for example "tests pass: 9 agents; reviewed parser: 1; built docs: 2"), not an "N agents agree" count, and nothing about correctness. Basis: prediscussion dissent raised solution rates even when wrong (Schulz-Hardt); "12 agents independently validated" is agreement without dissent. Prediction: more agents name criteria no one else cited; fewer `done` calls justified by "others validated". Measure: share of `done` reasons that cite another agent's validation; number of distinct criteria across reasons. Cost: 25 lines; needs reason text kept in a short field. Risk: agents copy the visible criteria and homogenise further; run only with reasons visible after an agent's own is committed (prior commit-then-reveal lever).

**M4. Neutral midpoint marker (information).** One line in the clock view at 50% of the run budget: "Half the time has passed. Elapsed X, remaining Y." Basis: Gersick's midpoint "alarm clock"; no instruction attached. Prediction: if the effect exists, a step change in what agents do around the midpoint (mode switch from exploring to consolidating, or more messages), without more early exits. Measure first, without building: in existing round traces, look at write and post rates and `done` times against the run's elapsed fraction; if `done` calls cluster before 50% or sharply after 80%, the clock is acting as a closure cue (De Grada) rather than a pacing cue. This costs only analysis. Build the marker only if no existing transition is visible. Cost: 5 lines.

**M5. Exit hold with a short overlap (tool; the riskiest).** `done` marks the agent as "leaving" for a fixed T (say 3 minutes) during which its claims remain, its inbox stays open and teammates see "X is handing over"; claims release at the end of T. Basis: Patterson strategies 18, 20, 21 (the leaver stays responsible until the update is complete; overlap; delay in critical work). Prediction: late questions to leavers get answered; nobody addresses a ghost. Measure: addressed posts answered by a leaver within T; posts to leavers after T. Cost: moderate; a clash with token budgets (a 12-agent swarm with all agents lingering); needs a hard timeout. Do not build unless M1 and M2 leave the "ghost addressee" problem unsolved.

## 7 What not to build

- **Assigned receivers or "relief" agents** (I-PASS names an incoming clinician; Patterson's relief dispatcher is a person). Assignment.
- **Incident-commander, scribe or liaison roles** (Bigley & Roberts; Faraj & Xiao). Hierarchy.
- **Assigned devil's advocate or red team.** An assigned role; authentic dissent is a different thing [memory]. The LLM devil's-advocate paper I came across is about humans aided by an LLM and says nothing about agents [abstract].
- **A "planning to leave" announcement** (anticipated change suppresses specialisation).
- **Longer or more elaborate `done` forms beyond a handful of required fields.** Keebler: 12+ items pass more but raise omissions and time; murmur's prompt-text record says wording does not bind.
- **Any signal of correctness in the divergence or arrival views.** Oracle-free means facts about activity and stated reasons only.

## 8 Three load-bearing claims (for the main session to verify)

1. **Read-back was not observed in any HRO setting.** Patterson et al. 2004, p. 127, Results, first paragraph after Table 3's lead-in: "With the exception of strategy 7 (having the incoming person read back information ...) and strategy 10 (updating information in the same order every time), every strategy was found in at least one setting". File: `https://www.interruptions.net/literature/Patterson-IntJQualHealthCare04.pdf`. This weakens the argument that murmur's `done` needs a receiver confirmation.
2. **Only the leader-declared exit broke Gersick's midpoint pattern.** Gersick 1988, p. 27: "Group G, dissolved with no prior warning (or protest) at its midpoint, was the only team that did not fit this pattern" and "Team G's leader unexpectedly pronounced the group's task complete at its midpoint"; p. 34 for the alarm-clock mechanism and the author's own hedge on midpoint timing. File: `https://web.mit.edu/curhan/www/docs/Articles/15341_Readings/Group_Dynamics/Gersick_1988_Time_and_transition.pdf`. This is why I do not read the clock as the cause of early exit.
3. **A written handover alone was no better than none.** Parke, Hobbs & Kanki, Results, "Errors in the Different Handover Conditions": paper alone 6.2 of 8 bolts with errors versus 3.5 face-to-face, and "cues alone" 4.8 (Ms = 3.6, 4, 4.8 for video, audio, cues alone); the Discussion says the control "did not produce as many errors as expected". File: `https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20110008267/downloads/20110008267.pdf`. Check that the cues-alone cell, not paper alone, is described as significantly different from face-to-face. The paper alone had significantly more errors than all other conditions except cues alone.
