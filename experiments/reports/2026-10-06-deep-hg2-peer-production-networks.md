# Peer production, self-managing teams and communication networks: what leaderless human groups do, and what it means for 12 agents on independent repositories

Scope: part 2 of the human-groups field. Part 1 (`2026-10-06-lit-human-groups-coordination.md`) covered MIA, cookie-licking, lazy consensus and handoffs; I do not repeat it. The failure modes FM1-FM8 are those of `2026-10-05-swarm-communication-diagnosis.md` (FM1 silent departure, FM2 "done" means my slice, FM3 stale claims, FM4 owner-gating, FM5 allocation blindness, FM6 echo, FM7 shared blind spot at the finish, FM8 rediscovered facts).

Reading log. About 15 sources read in full or in the relevant sections (Mockus 2002 draft PDF has no page numbers, cited by section). Abstract or secondary only: Kittur and Kraut 2008 (paywalled), Mason and Watts 2012, Lazer and Friedman 2007, Langfred 2004/2007, Stewart 2006, Magpili and Pazos 2018, Bernstein et al. 2016, Heylighen 2015, Li et al. 2020, Scholtes et al. 2016, Tan et al. 2020.

## 1. Headline

- Leaderless human peer production runs on four things, and none of them is messaging. (1) Self-assignment: 52.9-59.4% of all task-assignment messages in three FLOSS projects [full text, Crowston 2007, Table 3 (end matter), text p. 23]. (2) One person per task: about 80% of 106 tasks in Fire and Gaim had a single programmer, under 10% had two or more [full text, Howison WP pp. 17-18]. (3) Deferral of work that looks too big, rather than teaming up on it [full text, Howison WP pp. 18-19]. (4) A small core doing most of the writing (Apache: top 15 of 388 contributors wrote 83% of changes and 88% of added lines) coordinating implicitly through a public change stream [full text, Mockus §3.2.2, Fig. 1; §3.1.5].
- The same authors state the limit that matters for N=12: informal, ad hoc coordination holds up to about 10-15 people; beyond that, explicit process, ownership or inspection is needed [full text, Mockus §5, H1a/H2a]. Twelve agents sit at that edge.
- More explicit communication is not monotonically better. In Wikipedia, talk-page coordination helped articles with few editors and hurt articles with many, while concentrating the editing in a few people scaled [secondary: Kittur, Lee and Kraut 2009, p. 1496, quoting Kittur and Kraut 2008; CSCW paper not read]. Adding editors helps independent subtasks and hurts interdependent ones [full text, same paper, Table 2 and Table 4, pp. 1499, 1502].
- Timing and content of sharing matter more than amount. Intermittent sharing of solutions gave the high mean of constant sharing and the optimum-finding of no sharing [full text, Bernstein et al. 2018, pp. 8735-8736]. Clustering helps the search for facts and hurts the search for theories [full text, Shore et al. 2014, §1.2]. LLM agents copy neighbours more than humans (77-86% vs 54%) and regardless of payoff (r = 0.25 vs 0.69) [full text, He et al. 2026, §4.4].
- Abandonment is normal and recovery is slow: 16% of 1,932 popular GitHub projects lost their core developers, 41% of those were revived, in 86% of cases by one newcomer [full text, Avelino 2019, RQ1-RQ2]. Recovery is measured in months; every human timescale here must be rescaled to minutes.
- For murmur the useful transfer is not "add a structure". It is (a) derive allocation information from writes and show it constantly, (b) keep solution content at arm's length and share facts freely, (c) give "too big for me" a visible state instead of a held claim, and (d) never rely on a feedback signal. Prompt text is the weakest carrier of all of these.

## 2. Findings by topic

### 2.1 Apache and Mozilla (Mockus, Fielding, Herbsleb, TOSEM 2002) [full text, draft PDF, cited by section]

- Method: archival analysis of CVS change records and problem-report databases for Apache and Mozilla, compared with commercial projects. Apache: about 400 people contributed code, 249 to 6,092 code submissions and 182 to 695 fixes; 3,060 people filed 3,975 reports (§3.2.1).
- Concentration: the top 15 developers made more than 83% of the change requests and deltas, 88% of added lines, 91% of deleted lines; for defect fixes only 66% (§3.2.2, Fig. 1-2). A core of 10-15 writes new functionality, a group about ten times larger fixes defects, one about a hundred times larger reports them (H3, §5).
- Mechanism: no one assigns. Core developers pick problems "with which they are most familiar"; implicit "code ownership" exists but gives no veto; new core people take over areas "where the former maintainer is no longer interested" or "frontier" areas (§3.1.3). Every commit is auto-mailed to a list that most core developers read; review is commit-then-review for development releases (§3.1.5). A release manager volunteers, rotates, and "shakes the tree" (§3.1.6). A STATUS file in the repository lists open items (§3.1.2).
- Scaling limit: Mozilla modules needed core teams of 22-36 and answered with a single owner who must approve every change plus stricter inspection; productivity and defect density stayed good but "relatively long development intervals" appeared (§5, §6).

### 2.2 Self-assignment in FLOSS (Crowston, Li, Wei, Eseryel, Howison 2007; Crowston et al. 2012)

- Crowston 2007 [full text]: content analysis of mailing-list messages in three projects (Gaim, eGroupWare, Compiere), 101, 70 and 28 task-assignment messages; inter-rater agreement 0.81-0.89. Self-assignment was the most frequent mechanism in all three (59.4%, 52.9%, 57.1%; Table 3). Developers assign to others in 15-25% of their assignments, users almost never do: "a status hierarchy" (p. 22).
- Caveats written by the authors [full text, pp. 24-25]: much self-assignment is hedged ("if I have time"); because of ownership uncertainty "it is hard to track the status of developers' work, especially when it is self-assigned"; duplicate work is avoided by asking first ("can I call dibs on this?").
- Crowston 2012 review [full text, pp. 19-21 of the preprint; 184 papers from a search of 586]: self-assignment is "quite consistent" across the literature; modularity is "the most explicit" coordination mechanism; leaders emerge from sustained technical contribution and leadership is usually shared; restricted access to the core improves coordination (one study).

### 2.3 Open superposition (Howison and Crowston; working paper of MISQ 38(1):29-50, 2014) [full text, working paper]

- Method: four years of participant observation in BibDesk (13 listed developers, 5 consistently active), then archival replication in Fire and Gaim over one inter-release period (about 45 days), 106 tasks, then a formal model (pp. 4-19).
- Results: median and mean task duration under a week; about 80% of tasks had only one participant writing code, another ~10% a single author plus small polish by someone else, under 10% genuine co-work, and only one of those had any "management" actions (Fig. 3, pp. 17-18). 17 of 106 tasks were long-running; all had early "support" actions (a request, a post saying it is desirable), but all production work happened in short single-person episodes much later, typically triggered by an exogenous change such as a new library (Fig. 4, pp. 18-19). There was "no evidence of detailed planning, assignment or breakdown" for them, and planning appeared in a single task (p. 20).
- Theory: software is additive and layerable; each patch has its own payoff, so work can be laid in small independent layers over a shared base. Too-big work is deferred "until it is easier" because splitting it needs interdependent collaboration with risk of one half being wasted (pp. 20-33).
- The authors' own preconditions, which decide transfer: layerability, cheap instantiation and distribution, rewindability (changes can be undone, so neither trust nor control is needed, p. 39), and low financial cost. Their warning: when deadlines exist "the tactic of productive deferral is of reduced usefulness" and interpersonal dependencies must be borne (p. 40).
- N is tiny (three projects, one period for two of them) and it is a working paper marked not for citation. Treat as a well-argued description, not a measured effect.

### 2.4 Abandonment, takeover, truck factor, why projects fail

- Avelino et al. 2019 [full text, IEEE, arXiv 1906.08058]: 1,932 popular GitHub projects; 315 (16%) had their truck-factor developers leave and were "abandoned"; 128 (41%) survived through new core developers, in 86% of survivals via a single new developer, and newcomers (not existing contributors) supplied 48% of them (RQ2). Surviving projects had a median 56% of commits after the last departure, non-survivors 15% (Cliff's delta 0.79 for the percentage). Survey of 144 new core developers, 33 answers (24%): 53% were attracted because they used the project; barriers were lack of time (26% of 27), lack of experience, unfriendly maintainers, and, for 19%, "lack of access to the main repository" (Table VI).
- Avelino et al. 2016 [full text]: 133 systems, 65% have truck factor 2 or less; developers agreed with the identified authors in 84% but with the number in only 53%: a noisy metric.
- Coelho and Valente 2017 [full text]: 104 failed popular projects; reasons: usurped by competitor 27, obsolete 20, main contributor lacks time 18 or interest 18 (Table 2). Project-lifecycle reasons; little use for a 1-2 hour run.
- Stale work in the wild: almost half of "good first issues" are not solved by newcomers [secondary: Tan et al. 2020, snippet]; duplicate pull requests are tied to project hotness and slow review [abstract: Li et al. 2020]. I found no study that measures claim-to-finish rates for self-assigned issues.

### 2.5 Wikipedia: concentration versus communication (Kittur and Kraut 2008; Kittur, Lee and Kraut 2009)

- Kittur and Kraut 2008 [abstract, plus secondary]: adding editors improved quality only with suitable coordination; implicit coordination (a few editors do most of the work) helped more as the editor count rose; explicit coordination through talk-page communication did not. The 2009 paper restates it: "coordinating by direct communication among editors improved articles... with relatively few editors but actually harmed quality when many editors were involved" [secondary, full text of Kittur 2009, p. 1496]. I could not obtain the CSCW paper, so its N and effect sizes are not verified.
- Kittur, Lee and Kraut 2009 [full text, pp. 1495-1502]. Study 1: 20 articles ("American rock singers"), coverage (independent work) versus readability (interdependent). Number of editors (log2) had a positive effect on computer-coded coverage (b=0.38, SE 0.111, p<.01, row order read from a garbled table) and a marginal negative effect on readability (b=-0.276, SE 0.137); editor concentration (Gini) interacted positively with editor count for readability (b=2.869, SE 1.295, p<.05) and not for coverage. The authors warn about small N, non-expert coders and range restriction (p. 1499-1500). Study 2: 230 randomly sampled articles with 10,002 review comments, 4,601 suggestions, multilevel model; more editors meant a higher share of problems in interdependent aspects (b=0.016, SE 0.007), and concentration reduced that (interaction b=-0.127, SE 0.059, Table 4, p. 1502). The benefit of concentration came through the interaction (letting many contribute), not as a main effect: core editors "create a structure or environment for peripheral editors to flesh out" (p. 1502).
- Caveat: the outcome is review comments, not a verified quality score; correlational; Wikipedia editors are asynchronous and anonymous.

### 2.6 Team size and productivity (Scholtes et al. 2016; Sornette et al. 2014; Maillart and Sornette 2019)

- [Full text of Maillart and Sornette 2019; Scholtes and Sornette papers secondary.] Scholtes: 58 projects, 581,353 commits, 30,845 contributors; commits per active developer scaled as team^-0.24 (r2 = 0.16), total commits as team^-0.75 (r2 = 0.44): a Ringelmann effect. Sornette: 164 projects, superlinear production in bursts. Maillart and Sornette conclude superlinearity holds for no more than 30-50 simultaneous contributors and that team definitions and windows differ (295 vs 5 days). Both correlational. Unresolved at the scale of 12.

### 2.7 Experiments on communication networks

- Lazer and Friedman 2007 [abstract]: simulation; efficient networks win in the short run and lose in the long run on complex problems; an inverted U at intermediate horizons.
- Mason and Watts, PNAS 2012;109(3):764-769 [abstract; the experiment as described in He et al. 2026]: 16 participants per group, 8 networks with 3 neighbours each, 15 rounds, a 2-D payoff landscape; shorter-path networks earned more, contradicting the simulation. The paper shows each player's own payoff, i.e. an oracle.
- Shore, Bernstein and Lazer (Collective Intelligence 2014 extended abstract of the Org. Sci. 2015 paper) [full text, extended abstract]: 417 people, 1,120 person-rounds, four 16-person networks (caveman = four cliques of four, hierarchy, rewired caveman, ring lattice) on a whodunit with clues and theories. Clustered networks found less redundant facts but registered fewer unique theories and copied more; the two most clustered networks scored highest overall; no individual gained from a clustered position. Their reading: "structures that are good now may be bad later". Only the extended abstract; no coefficients.
- Bernstein, Shore and Lazer 2018 [full text, pp. 8734-8739]: triads solving a 25-city travelling-salesman problem 17 times; constant, intermittent (visible every third round) or no sharing; 514 trials, 26,214 solutions (the number of people is not stated in the pages I read). The optimum was found in 33.3% (constant), 48.3% (intermittent), 44.1% (none) of trials (p. 8735); mean solution quality of intermittent equalled constant and beat none (+0.351 log distance, p<.001); unique solutions per triad 30.5 / 27.5 / 21.4. A "storage" feature that reloads one's own best solution cut unique solutions to 0.706x in the intermittent arm and removed most of the benefit (p. 8736). Caveats: lab, students, 50-second rounds, a task where everyone saw the score of each submitted route (an oracle), and a one-sentence statement that other task types (additive or smooth) would not reward this (p. 8737).
- Almaatouq et al. 2020 [full text, PNAS]: groups of 12, N=719 and 702 on Mechanical Turk, 20 rounds of estimating a scatter-plot correlation. Dynamic networks beat static ones by 17% (individual) and 18% (group) error reduction, but only with performance feedback; no-feedback gave no such gain, self-feedback gave 7% and a non-significant 11% (E1, E2). Needs ground truth; murmur has none.
- Almaatouq et al. 2021 [full text, PNAS]: 1,200 recruited, 828 in phase 2, groups of three (196 valid interacting groups) with chat, on a room-assignment optimisation task with graded complexity. Groups were more efficient than the best individual only at high complexity; they lost time on excess exploration after the best solution (T3) and on reaching consensus to submit (T4). A direct analogue of FM7 and finish-time waste.
- He, Kuhlman and Deng 2026 [full text, arXiv 2607.14574]: 2,400 games of 16 agents on the 8 Mason-Watts graphs; gpt-oss-120b, medium reasoning. With default prompts 7,677 of 7,680 first-round choices were the grid centre (50,50): identical first picks. A single sentence telling agents to randomise the first round raised collective payoff by more than three times the whole topology effect and revealed a topology effect that was otherwise absent. Copying rate over rounds 2-15: 86% (randomised LLM), 77% (default LLM), 54% (humans); correlation between an agent's copying and its payoff: 0.25 (default LLM), 0.42, 0.69 humans; spatial diversity fell to 2-3 cells against 10 for humans. One model, one prompt template, synchronous play, one task type.

### 2.8 Self-managing teams and organisations

- Langfred 2004 [abstract]: 71 self-managing MBA teams; high trust helped when individual autonomy was low and hurt when it was high, because trusting members monitor less. Langfred 2007 [abstract]: 35 teams, longitudinal; conflict lowers trust, and teams then cut autonomy and loosen interdependence inefficiently.
- Stewart 2006 [abstract]: meta-analysis of 93 studies; more autonomy and intrateam coordination go with higher performance, depending on task; leadership, especially empowering leadership, helps. Magpili and Pazos 2018 [abstract level only]: systematic review of input factors; I did not obtain details.
- Lee 2019 dissertation (chapter 2 is the Lee and Edmondson 2017 review) [full text, selected chapters]. Chapter 3: a 125-person firm (CashCo) under Holacracy coordinated without managers through role definitions that were formal, visible to everyone and easy to revise (pp. 95-98 of the thesis); even there "Lead Links" could assign roles and set priorities but not override work decisions, and informal influence hierarchies persisted (Zappos, Valve examples). Chapter 4: a 12-month controlled field experiment in a government agency, 131 versus 134 employees in 39 groups: no main effect of radical decentralisation on empowerment, engagement or satisfaction; effects were positive for high performers and those interested in it and negative for others (Discussion, p. 145 ff.). The outcomes are experience measures, not output.
- Swarming and mob programming: no controlled study found.

## 3. Answers

(a) How leaderless groups allocate work and recover abandoned work, and at what cost. They self-assign from a public stream of requests (about 55% of assignment messages), usually one person per task, and defer what is too large until a trigger arrives; no breakdown or assignment is observed for those tasks. Orphaned areas are recovered by users or newcomers who need the thing and gain commit rights, in 41% of abandoned projects, in 86% of those through one person [full text, Crowston 2007; Howison WP; Avelino 2019]. Cost: months, not minutes; and the process depends on a single volunteer appearing. The "dibs" check ("can I take this?") and the status file are the only active hand-offs described.

(b) When explicit communication helps and hurts. It helps with few contributors and independent subtasks and for sharing facts; it hurts with many contributors on interdependent work (Kittur, secondary for the 2008 claim, full text for the 2009 interaction), and when it makes everyone adopt the same theory (Shore). Intermittent sharing of solutions beats constant sharing at equal average quality while keeping exploration (Bernstein, with a visible score). LLM agents copy more than humans and independent of payoff (He 2026), so for them constant visibility of others' solutions is more dangerous, not less.

(c) What open superposition implies for 12 agents on independent repositories. Each repo is already a base that one agent can lay layers on; independence between repos means tasks are low-interdependence, which is the regime in which Kittur finds more workers helpful and Howison finds single-person episodes sufficient. So the prediction is: one agent per repo layer at a time is the natural unit and co-working on a repo is rarely needed. The risk Howison names is deferral, not interference: with a run clock, too-big work needs a visible state and a taker.

(d) What the self-managing evidence says about minimal structures. Four things recur: (1) a public, derived record of who is doing what (Apache change mailing list and STATUS file; Holacracy's visible role map); (2) a way to take work without permission (self-assignment, authority over one's role); (3) a defined, cheap way to leave or hand over a piece (Avelino's barrier "push access"; Lee's revisable roles); (4) for N beyond about 10-15, some mechanism of ownership or inspection (Mockus), which human projects implement with owners that hold a veto, i.e. with gatekeeping that murmur must not copy as a person. Autonomy without monitoring has a measured cost (Langfred 2004), so the monitoring has to be mechanical rather than a duty. None of the sources shows that a manager is necessary; Lee's field experiment shows that removing managers does not by itself improve outcomes (no main effect, experience measures only).

## 4. What transfers to LLM agents and what does not

| Finding | Depends on (humans) | Agents lack | Agents have |
|---|---|---|---|
| Self-assignment from a stream (Crowston) | Reputation, interest, spare time | Intrinsic motive, reputation | No fear of looking incompetent; a claim costs one call; they read all posts at the next tool result (4-5 s median) |
| Single-person tasks, deferral (Howison) | Layerable artifact, rewind, no deadline, learning motive | Free time, "wait until easier" | Rewind through git; cheap instantiation; but a hard run clock, so deferral expires |
| Core of 10-15 does 80% (Mockus) | Skill and trust build up over months | Months of history | All start with equal skill; concentration can be chosen by who enters first (staggered entry) rather than earned |
| Implicit coordination by a public change stream | Reading the mailing list daily | Habit of reading it | The tool result can carry it for free |
| Talk helps few, hurts many (Kittur 2008) | Coordination cost and loafing | Loafing, fatigue | Reading cost is tokens, not morale; the harm shows as echo (FM6) and token spend |
| Intermittent sharing (Bernstein) | Humans copy partially and noisily | Noisy partial copying | Copy more, convergent first moves (He 2026); the benefit of breaks should be larger, but the study had a score, and murmur has none |
| Facts vs figuring (Shore) | Clustered people share clues and theories | Clues are cheap to share | Facts (a test needs network, a repo layout) are exactly FM8; theories are what must stay diverse |
| Dynamic rewiring (Almaatouq 2020) | Feedback with ground truth | Ground truth | None, without an oracle |

## 5. Where the field disagrees or results are fragile

- Efficient versus inefficient networks: Lazer and Friedman (simulation) predicted that sparse networks win on hard problems; Mason and Watts (humans) found the opposite; Barkoczi and Galesic (reported in the earlier report) explained it by the copy rule; Shore et al. say it depends on whether the search is over facts or theories; He et al. find no complexity by path-length interaction in LLM agents (all F < 1) and no topology effect at the final round. No consensus, and most of it uses a payoff oracle.
- Team size and productivity: Scholtes (Ringelmann, scaling exponents with r2 0.08-0.44) against Sornette (superlinear); Maillart and Sornette say both can hold at different sizes. Nothing here is controlled.
- Concentration: Kittur's Study 1 rests on 20 articles; Study 2 uses review comments as outcome; the 2008 paper with the N-dependence claim was not accessible.
- Superposition: three projects, one author's participation, working paper; the authors say it fails under deadlines.
- Self-managing organisations: Lee finds no main effect; the cases (Zappos, CashCo) are single firms and outcomes are self-reports; Holacracy itself keeps assignable roles, so "no manager" is not "no assignment".

## 6. Levers

Class labels: I information the agent sees, T tool mechanics, P prompt text. Oracle-free means nothing reveals correctness; all are keyed to events (writes, claims, clock), not to checks. Prompt text alone: murmur's record is that it rarely moves behaviour, so each lever is stated as I or T first.

L1. Derived work map in every tool result (I). One line per repo: editors in the last 10 minutes by name, minutes since the last write by anyone, whether any agent has `done` with an unfinished remark. No quality signal. Source: Apache change stream and STATUS file (Mockus §3.1.2, §3.1.5), Kittur 2009 (concentration visible), Heylighen (the trace is the medium). Targets FM5, FM1, FM3. Prediction: fewer repos with no writer for a long gap, and fewer repos with 3+ editors while another has none (the wasmi 4 editors at 1.0 versus dasel 0 at 0.22 pattern). Measure from events.jsonl: edit/write calls per repo per 5-minute window, gaps without a write, entropy of writes across repos, against the e18-r1-style baseline. Cost: small; it extends the existing team line or status line from round 20. Does not need a manager.

L2. `done` with leftover items that become a visible "open" list (T). Replace free-text reasons by a call that takes `unfinished: [repo or slice]`; any listed item shows in the work map as open, not claimed. Source: Howison (deferral with a visible request state), Crowston 2007 (status of self-assigned work is untrackable), Avelino (cheap access and a hand-over path). Targets FM2, FM1. Prediction: the 35 of 58 partial-scope dones become visible items; a second agent starts such a repo within minutes. Measure: share of dones with non-empty unfinished, time from `done` to the first write by another agent in the listed repo. Cost: schema change in `done`; the earlier handoff report already proposes required fields, so this is the same mechanism and I add only the open-list view. Exists in real work (a TODO or issue list).

L3. A "too big for me" state in the task list in place of a claim (T). Release on `done` already exists when the task list is on; add a state `deferred` that shows the task, the agent's one-line reason and how long it has waited, and that any agent can take without asking. Source: Howison (deferral is how humans handle too-big work, with no planning). Prediction: fewer long-lived claims without writes, fewer FM3 orphan episodes (e18-r1 expr, e19-r0 expr). Measure: claim-to-first-write latency and claims with no write within T minutes; takes of deferred items. Cost: small. Not a manager: the agent chooses to defer.

L4. Share facts, delay solutions (I/T). Two channels: a facts note (environment, repo layout, "go test needs network") attached at once; the contents of other agents' diffs and plans attached only after a delay or on request. Source: Shore (facts vs theories), Bernstein (intermittent), He (LLM copying is positional). Targets FM8 and FM6. Prediction: FM8 episodes fall; echo posts fall; this is least certain for coding because each agent works on a separate repo and little solution sharing is needed anyway. Measure: repeated discovery of the same fact (the three hits on Kong download), post classification, first-pick overlap. Cost: needs a classification at write time (agent labels a post as fact); mislabels are possible. Round 20 knowledge: ST-tail attaches the latest post of each teammate and ST-file has no board; neither separates facts from solutions, so this is a new axis.

L5. First-pick dispersion (I, with a prompt sentence as the cheap variant) (T/P). He et al. show that default LLM agents all choose the same first move and that a sentence about randomising the first round moves collective outcome more than the network does. Murmur's stagger (one entry per minute) is a mechanical version; measure first-claim collisions (two agents claiming one repo within a minute), expected rare. If not rare, a tool that proposes the repo with the fewest writers to a new agent (derived from L1) is the mechanical fix. This is the one documented case where a prompt sentence beat structure, but it was a one-shot simultaneous choice; do not generalise.

L6. Rotating volunteer "sweeper" in the role menu for the last N minutes (P/T, untested). Apache's release manager volunteers, rotates, and "shakes the tree" (Mockus §3.1.6). Self-picked from the existing role menu, so allowed. Targets FM7 and the finish. Prediction: the number of runs where 10+ agents finish within a few minutes without anyone looking at the weakest repo falls. Measure: writes in repos with the lowest activity in the last 20 minutes. Needs a trigger at minute N, a clock event, not a check. Risk: if the volunteer is chosen by the board's first poster, that is de facto assignment; the pick must be by self-entry only.

What needs a manager and is therefore excluded: Mozilla module owners who must approve every change (and the FM4 owner-gating they would cause), Holacracy Lead Links assigning roles, Apache votes and committer lists, Linux maintainers. Derived views and self-service states are the non-hierarchical substitutes.

## 7. What not to build

- Performance-based rewiring or reputation weighting (Almaatouq 2020): needs accuracy feedback, which here is an oracle.
- Constant visibility of everyone's solutions (Bernstein, Shore, He): a free board is large-sample conformity.
- Hard claim expiry or stale-bot closing: Part 1 found soft annotation safer; nothing here supports hard expiry.
- Module-owner approval or veto (Mozilla): it is the owner-gating of FM4.
- Planning or breakdown meetings for the swarm: Howison finds none in human work; Kittur finds communication hurts at larger N (secondary).
- Trust prompts ("trust your teammates"): Langfred's cost comes from lowered monitoring; monitor mechanically (L1) or not at all.
- Pair or mob mechanics as default for independent repos, and a truck-factor dashboard (the metric matches developers' own view about half the time): no evidence of benefit here.
